Method & limits

How a temperature is recovered from a picture

NASA GIBS serves science layers as pre-coloured images, not numbers. For every layer NASA also publishes the exact palette as a ColorMap document, mapping each RGB triplet to the physical interval it represents.

This page fetches that palette, builds a reverse lookup, and inverts each pixel back to a value — degrees Celsius for temperature, unitless for NDVI, persons per square kilometre for population. Units are read from NASA's metadata rather than assumed. Palette entries marked transparent or no-data decode to nothing, which is how cloud and fill pixels are screened out.

Why several dates instead of one

A single thermal image is mostly cloud. The analysis samples the same grid across several dates drawn from the area's hottest season — June to September in the northern hemisphere, December to March in the southern — and takes the per-cell median of whatever decodes. One bad day cannot move the result, and a date that returns nothing is skipped rather than failing the run.

Where the number comes from

Across every valid cell, land surface temperature is regressed against NDVI using the Theil–Sen estimator, the median of all pairwise slopes, which resists the outliers that water bodies and cloud edges introduce. That slope is this area's measured cooling rate, in degrees per unit of vegetation index. R² from ordinary least squares is reported alongside so you can see how strong the relationship actually is.

A cell's shade debt is its vegetation shortfall against the local 75th percentile, multiplied by that slope. Sites are ranked by degree-persons — avoidable heat times the people living in the cell — so that a hot empty cell does not outrank a slightly cooler one where thousands live.

Tree counts come from fractional vegetation cover, FVC = ((NDVI − NDVI_soil) / (NDVI_veg − NDVI_soil))², after Carlson & Ripley (1997), converted to canopy area and divided by 50 m² per established tree.

What this cannot tell you

Land surface temperature is not air temperature. It is the temperature of the ground itself, measured by a thermal sensor, and on a summer afternoon asphalt runs far hotter than the air above it. Treat these values as a comparison between places, not as what a thermometer would read.

The grid is about one kilometre. MODIS thermal resolution identifies neighbourhoods, not individual streets. This is triage — where to look first — and not a planting plan.

Correlation, not proof. Vegetation and temperature move together for reasons beyond shade: water, elevation, building density and surface material all play a part. The measured slope is a defensible local association, not a guarantee that planting a tree delivers exactly that cooling.

Population is modelled. SEDAC's gridded population is itself an estimate, redistributed from census units.
