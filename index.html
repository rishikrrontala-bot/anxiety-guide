A Teen's Guide to Understand on How to Manage Anxiety

By Rishik Rontala

Anxiety is something that every teenager experiences, but most don’t know how much it affects daily life. It could be the pressure of taking a test, trying out for a new club, or even simply managing friendships, anxiety can be too much. But for some anxiety goes beyond the occasional worry and can impact their daily lives in ways they might not even realize. Understanding how to manage anxiety is not only essential for the present, but will be an important skill for going through future challenges.

31.9%

Did you know by the time teens turn 18 over 31.9% of them will be diagnosed with an anxiety disorder according to the Anxiety and Depression Association of America? That’s almost one in three teens! Yet, many teens don’t know how to handle it or where to turn for help when they need it. As someone who dealt with an anxiety disorder during high school, I know how overwhelming it can feel to juggle school, friends, and future expectations. Let’s explore what anxiety is, how to manage it, and what resources are available to help you thrive.

01 — Understanding It

What Exactly Is Anxiety?

Anxiety is an unpleasant feeling that everyone experiences sometimes. You might describe it as feeling very nervous or “wound up”.

General anxiety symptoms can last for extended periods of time – it could go on for a couple of weeks or longer. It really is more than just being nervous.

You might have anxious thoughts about yourself, other people, and how you think other people feel about you. You might also have anxious thoughts about the world around you and what is coming in the future.

Why is anxiety so common among teens today?

The issues teens face today are issues that other generations did not have to confront:

Academic pressurecompetition for college has become more competitive than ever before, so the pressure on teens increased too.
Social Media OverloadConstant Comparison and Fear of Missing Out (FOMO)
Uncertain futureConcerns about climate change, economic instability, and world events
Reduced sleep timeLate night studying and screen time reducing sleep.
Overscheduled LivesBalancing School, Extracurriculars, Work and their social life.
02 — Notice The Pattern

Patterns of unhelpful thinking

Knowing the common patterns that unhelpful thoughts follow can help you recognise and challenge them before they have a negative effect on the things you do.

Here are some common patterns of unhelpful thinking:

Catastrophizing

People often catastrophize when they’re anxious. This means the anxiety makes them feel like something that’s happened is far worse than it really is.

Black and white thinking

People often see things as black or white when they’re feeling anxious – there’s no ‘in between’.

“I failed that exam – I’m going to fail my whole degree.”

Emotional reasoning

This means treating emotions as if they’re facts.

“I feel anxious – something bad is going to happen.”

Must and should statements

People often live by fixed rules for themselves about what they “should” do and how they “should” feel, and judge themselves harshly if they don’t meet them.

“I should be able to cope.”
When people think like this, they’re being critical of themselves, which makes the anxiety worse.

Jumping to conclusions

People often assume they know what others are thinking, and the assumptions are usually about the future – there are frightening “what ifs” running through their minds.

“I’m starting a new job tomorrow – no one’s going to speak to me.”

Over-generalising

Based on one isolated incident, people with this thinking pattern assume all future events will follow a similar pattern. It becomes hard to see a negative event as a one-off.

Real-life example

Let's meet Alex, not a real person, but a combination of experiences for what many teenagers have gone through or are going through. Alex is a sophomore in high school who used to love school. Recently, however, it feels like the week prior to exams is too much to bear. Alex cannot sleep, he wakes up every morning nauseous, and thinks about the worst case scenarios in his head. There was this one very stressful week when he did not go to the cafeteria at all because the thought of sitting amongst friends was overwhelming.

Sound familiar?

According to research from the NIMH, this is the way your body is letting you know when the level of stress has just become too high. The good thing? It can be a good thing if you recognize it and work on it.

03 — Small Shifts

Making Your life better

Doing something you enjoy will give you less time to spend worrying, and make you feel better overall. Suggestions:

  • Do some exercise, such as going swimming or cycling.
  • Spend time with a friend or family member.
  • Read a book.
  • Watch your favourite TV show.
  • Go to the cinema.
  • Do something creative, like drawing or painting.
  • Have a bath or shower.

You could do a web search for events happening in your area and see if there’s anything you would be interested in. Just remember it’s important to pace yourself – you do not have to do everything at once. You will know what works best for you.

04 — Right Now

Feeling calmer:

Breathing exercises can help you feel calmer and reduce your anxiety.

The controlled breathing technique involves focusing on and slowing down your breathing. It’s particularly helpful if you feel dizzy or light-headed when you get anxious.

in · 1 · 2 · 3

Follow the circle: in for three seconds, hold for two, out for three. Try a few rounds while you read.

  • Get into a comfortable position – you could lie on your bed, or sit on a comfortable chair.
  • Try to breathe in a steady rhythm. Perhaps try to breathe in for three seconds, hold this breath for two seconds, and then breathe out for three seconds. It can be helpful to count as you do this – for example, “in, 1,2,3… out, 1,2,3”.
  • Repeat the steady breathing for a few minutes.
  • You should soon begin to feel more relaxed. If you were feeling dizzy then this should also get better after a few minutes.

We have a number of other breathing and relaxation exercises on NIMH information that can help with anxiety and stress. Try doing one of these when you’re feeling anxious.

05 — Daily Habits
Manage Screen Time:
  • Try putting your phone away an hour before bed, which also helps you sleep well.
  • Unfollow and stop using anything that makes you feel worse about yourself.
  • Remember that not everything in social media is not real life.
Prioritize Sleep:
  • Try to get 8-10 hours of sleep
  • Create a calming bedtime routine.
  • Keep your room cool and dark.
  • Avoid caffeine for a couple of hours before going to sleep.
Connect with Others:
  • Spend time with people who make you feel good.
  • Talk to friends who understand.
  • Join a club or activity where you feel comfortable.
  • Sometimes just being around others helps you aloy.
06 — When It's More

When to Reach Out for Help

While it may not seem like it right now, one of the bravest things you can do is ask for help. Early intervention is key in managing anxiety long-term. This is according to the Anxiety and Depression Association of America.

You should contact a trusted adult in the following circumstances:

  • Schooling, friendships, and hobbies that you typically enjoy are being affected by anxiety
  • You're avoiding important situations due to fear or worry
  • Physical manifestations are affecting your everyday life
  • Are you using alcohol or drugs to cope with things?
  • You're having thoughts of hurting yourself

Who can help:

School counselor

They are trained to help you with these kinds of issues and they keep it confidential too.

Parent or guardian

Just reach out and talk to parents about how stressful you are feeling and they will try their best to help you out.

Other trusted adults

Teacher, coach, relative you think can help you or even Doctors who are trained for this.

08 — For The Grown-Ups

As a parent, what can I do to help?

  • Acknowledge your teen’s fears and emotions. Don’t ignore it or dismiss it by saying, “You have a good life. You shouldn’t worry about that.” We need to take their emotions seriously and be ready to listen. Don’t meet them with too many questions, let them talk, and sit in silence.
  • Remind them that other kids are anxious too. Try to avoid labeling our teens with negative labels such as “shy” or “anxious.”
  • Start with a level of exposure — slowly working up to the worry, but providing positive reinforcement for tasks and social activities they are worried about.
  • Give your child love and empathy.
  • Make time to talk to your child every day without distractions.
  • Encourage healthy diet, physical activities and good sleep habits.
  • Strong parent-teenager relationships are good for young people’s mental health and are protective.
  • If you need to seek more help, reach out to your pediatrician or trusted psychologist/counselor for additional support. School counselors can also be helpful.

As an educator, what can I do to help?

  • Create an anxiety aware classroom.
  • Provide breaks to them, when needed.
  • Be flexible about participation( as not everyone is comfortable speaking in front of everyone.
  • Observe any changes in the way they act or behave.
  • Let school counselors help them.
Don't Worry

Don’t Worry

Anxiety is real, it is common, and it is nothing to be ashamed about. These symptoms-the racing heart, the sleepless nights, the constant thought of worry-are your body ways to signal that it needs support.

The coping strategies we've discussed here-breathing exercises, movement, journaling, managing screen time, prioritizing sleep, and reaching out to others-are practices that the research shows actually work. Not every tool will work for you, and that's OK. Try different strategies and stay with what helps.

Most of all, know when to ask for help. Whether it is talking with a school counselor, opening up to a parent, or calling a helpline, seeking support is a sign of self-awareness and courage-not weakness.

You've Got This

Your mental health is just as important as your physical health. Just as you wouldn't ignore a broken arm, you shouldn't ignore your anxiety. Help is available, and millions of teens who have felt exactly like you are feeling now have found ways to manage their anxiety and lead normal, productive, and happy lives.

You deserve to feel good. You deserve to be supported. And you're stronger than you think.

To get one thing out of this article, I encourage you to remember this: you’re not alone, there is help available and things can get better.

Take that first step today. Talk to someone you trust. Try a new coping tool. Go to one of the resources listed above. You will thank yourself in the future.
