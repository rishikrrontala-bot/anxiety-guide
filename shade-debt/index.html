The heat a neighbourhood owes to its missing trees.

Shade Debt reads NASA satellite imagery in your browser, recovers real surface temperatures from it, and ranks the places where planting removes the most heat from the most people.

Within one city, one block runs hotter than the next. That is not weather.

It tracks where the trees are. Shade debt is the part of that difference you could take back — and this measures it, in degrees, from orbit.

Three layers, one grid, one question.

  1. 01

    Surface temperature

    MODIS thermal, day and night. The night band matters most — heat illness accumulates when the dark does not let a body cool.

  2. 02

    Vegetation

    NDVI on the same grid, composited across a season so that a single cloudy day cannot decide the answer.

  3. 03

    People

    SEDAC gridded population, because a hot empty lot is not the same problem as a hot street where a thousand people sleep.

Priority is avoidable heat multiplied by the people it is avoidable for. Degree-persons. No weights to argue about.

How a temperature is recovered from a picture.

NASA serves these layers as coloured images, not numbers. It also publishes the exact palette for each one — every RGB triplet mapped to the physical interval it represents.

Shade Debt fetches that palette, stitches the map tiles into a canvas, reads the pixels back, and inverts each one into degrees Celsius. The units come from NASA's own metadata, never from an assumption baked into this page.

Palette entries marked transparent or no-data decode to nothing. So the cloud mask falls out of the method for free — a clouded pixel simply never becomes a measurement.

Live NASA tiles, decoded in this page as you read it. If your network blocks them these panels stay empty — they will not show you something invented.

Calibrated here. Not borrowed.

The cooling slope is measured from the study area's own cells. Ordinary least squares is not good enough for that job: water bodies and cloud edges throw outliers that drag the line off. Shade Debt uses Theil–Sen — the median of every pairwise slope.
